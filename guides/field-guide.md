# The Saturday Board — a field guide (V3)

*Can a market crowd out-think the capitol?*

Every Saturday we put up a board at the farmers market and ask one real
question — an issue the capitol has already debated and voted on, though we
don't say which way the vote went. Over the day, one visitor at a time spends
about five minutes at the board. Nobody runs the discussion. Nobody is in
charge of the answer. By closing time the board has settled on one, and we
compare it with what the politicians chose.

No single visitor solves the problem. The *board* solves it. Each person adds
a little, trims a little, and moves on; the answer grows out of many small,
honest contributions. That only works if everyone follows the same short
procedure — so the procedure below matters, including its order.

---

## The board

Two long strips, one above the other, each a row of **stacks** of sticky
notes. New notes go **on top of** old ones, covering them, so a stack only
ever takes up the space of one note however tall it grows. The number in the
top corner of each top note says how tall the stack is underneath it.

1. **The Wall of Why** (pink stacks, upper strip) — where it hurts. Every
   visitor's personal stake in the issue lives here. One stack per distinct
   pain; the number on top is how many people have said it. This is the
   problem, told by the people who have it. Nothing pink is ever taken down.
2. **The Field** (yellow and orange stacks, lower strip) — the ideas. One
   stack per idea; the top note is the idea's current wording, and the number
   on top is how much the crowd has built on it. Stacks that are close in
   meaning sit **next to each other**; a **hand's-width gap** between stacks
   means "these are different kinds of answer, not versions of one." Position
   along the strip carries no rank — there is no good end and no bad end.
3. **The Archive** (a box, with a logbook) — notes the crowd has taken down.
   Nothing is ever binned or torn up. A lifted note goes in the box and gets a
   line in the log: what it said, which stack it came off, and when.

**Materials:** pink, yellow and orange sticky notes; markers; a timer; the
steward's logbook; small letter cards (A, B, C…) to label stacks; and the
issue card — one paragraph stating this week's question neutrally, with no
options listed.

---

## One visit — six steps, in this order

A visit takes about five minutes. The order is not decoration; the method
fails if the steps are shuffled — above all, if you read the Field before you
have written your own idea.

### 1. Say where it hurts *(pink, ~45 seconds)*

The steward reads the issue card aloud. You write one pink note: how does this
issue touch your life? What does it cost you, or someone you know?

Then look at the Wall of Why — the top note of each pink stack. If one already
says your pain, put yours **on top of it** and write the stack's number plus
one in the corner. If none does, start a new stack and write **1**.

*Don't look at the Field yet.*

### 2. First instinct *(yellow, ~60 seconds)*

Before you've read a single idea from anyone else, write your gut answer to
the question. Half-formed is fine. Obvious is fine. There is no wrong answer at
this step — a "bad" idea still teaches the board something, and the board
will sort it out later.

### 3. Post it *(~15 seconds)*

Now look at the Field for the first time — but your note is already finished.
**Post it exactly as written: no edits, no rewrites, not a word**, even if you
spot a note you like better than your own. Every yellow note on the board is
a first instinct; that is the point.

Where it goes is a filing question, not a debate, and it is *yours* to
answer:

- If a stack's top note says what yours says, put yours **on top of it** and
  write the stack's number plus one in the corner.
- If nothing says it, start a new stack: **1** in the corner. Put it **beside
  the stack it is most like**. If it isn't like anything on the board, leave
  a hand's-width gap.

Nobody chooses their own stack's standing. A stack grows because *other*
people build on it, never because its author did.

### 4. Read *(~75 seconds)*

Read the top note of every stack on both strips, with the number in its
corner. That is the whole board as it stands: every pain and how many share
it, every idea in its current wording and how much the crowd has built on it.

You don't need to dig into the stacks. What's underneath is history, and it
is in the numbers.

### 5. Add *(orange and pink, ~60 seconds — both optional)*

Two things you *may* add, one of each:

- **One orange note.** If someone else's idea makes you think "almost — but
  I'd say it like this," write your version. Then either put it **on top of
  their stack** (a rewording or refinement — the stack's number plus one), or,
  if it is a real variation, start a **new stack right beside theirs** (number
  1). Any stack is fair game, not just the tall ones. Judge with the Wall of
  Why: is your version better for pains that aren't yours?
- **One more pink note** — but only for a pain that is **not on the wall
  yet**. It starts a new pink stack at 1. If your pain is already up there,
  it has your number in step 1 and that is enough.

### 6. Trim *(~45 seconds)*

You have **three strokes**. A stroke is a tally mark in the bottom corner of
a **top note in the Field** — the note you can see, never one underneath.
Put a stroke on a note if that idea, as currently worded, would make what is
written on the Wall of Why worse.

Limits: no strokes on a stack you have added to today; no more than two
strokes on one note; pink never gets strokes.

**Three strokes and it comes off.** If your stroke is the third, lift the
note and hand it to the steward for the Archive. The note underneath is now
the top: taking a note off rejects *that wording*, not the idea. An idea is
gone only when its stack is empty.

Judge with the pink wall, not just your own taste. The question for every
stroke is: *would this idea actually add to what's written on the Wall of
Why — including the pains that aren't mine?*

### If others are at the stall: the table

When two or three visitors are at the board at once, after everyone has
finished step 6 they may talk for two minutes about what's on the board — then
each may place **one** extra stroke. Talk comes after the individual steps,
never before: the board wants your own instinct first and your considered
judgement second, not an echo of the loudest voice.

## The steward

The steward keeps the board tidy and never touches the answer:

- **Never** suggests an idea, hints, files a note for anyone, or places a
  stroke. Ever. If a visitor can't decide where a note goes, the steward says
  "it's your call" — and means it.
- Reads the issue card, keeps time, hands out notes, stamps hands (one visit
  per person per Saturday).
- Gives each new stack its letter card.
- **Slides stacks along the strip to make room** — keeping their order, and
  keeping the gaps. Never reorders them, never merges two stacks, never
  splits one, never moves a note from one stack to another.
- Takes lifted notes into the Archive and writes the log line.
- Once an hour, fills in the logbook tally: new yellow notes, new orange
  notes (on-top and beside, separately), new pink stacks, strokes placed,
  notes lifted, number of stacks, and the leading neighbourhood.

---

## Reading the answer

At closing time, for every stack in the Field, add up **its number plus the
numbers of the stacks immediately to its left and right** — not across a gap.
That total is the stack's **neighbourhood**. The biggest neighbourhood wins.
Its middle stack's top note is the headline; the neighbours are the answer's
family of variations — read them together as one position. The steward reads
the numbers out loud so anyone can check.

Note what the rule is *not*: the answer is not simply the tallest stack. A
tall stack standing alone — one nobody could rephrase or vary and still
agree with — loses to a stack of the same height with living neighbours. An
answer the whole market can live with beats an answer one group loves.

**Settling early:** if the leading neighbourhood has not changed for a full
hour, the board is done — it can be read out before closing time.

---

## The scoreboard

Each week, record three things side by side:

| | |
|---|---|
| **The board's answer** | the winning neighbourhood, headline and family |
| **The capitol's answer** | what was actually voted, revealed only at close |
| **What happened since** | how the real decision worked out, where known |

Over the weeks, that table is the experiment: a running answer to whether an
open crowd, five minutes at a time, finds better answers than the people
elected to find them.

---

## Why the odd rules? *(the short version)*

- **Instinct before reading** keeps each idea independent. If people read
  first, the board stops hearing a hundred minds and starts hearing its own
  echo. It is also the only way a *genuinely different kind* of answer ever
  gets onto the board: nobody reaches it by rewording what's already there.
- **Pain before ideas** because nobody can judge solutions to a problem they
  haven't heard. The pink wall is the problem statement, written by the
  people who own it — and the reason a visitor can trim an idea for a harm
  they will never personally feel.
- **On top, never at the bottom** because an idea's standing should be the
  amount the crowd has built on it, and nothing else. There is no climbing and
  no voting. To lift an idea you have to *add* to it.
- **Read only the tops** because that is all anyone can honestly read in five
  minutes, and a small read that is actually done beats a big read that is
  faked. The numbers carry the history.
- **Three strokes, two per person** so that no visitor — however confident —
  can take anything down alone. The board's opinion is only allowed to change
  a little at a time.
- **Lifting reveals** because rejecting a wording should not destroy the idea
  under it. Only a stack that has been trimmed to nothing is gone.
- **Neighbours count** because how many *near-agreements* an idea collects is
  information. Ideas that survive being slightly rephrased are the ones worth
  trusting; the gap rule stops unrelated ideas borrowing each other's weight.
- **The Archive, never the bin** because knowing what the crowd let go — and
  when — is part of the answer. Nothing stops the next visitor writing the
  same idea again as their own first instinct.

That's the whole method. Put up the board, hold the order, trust the crowd.
